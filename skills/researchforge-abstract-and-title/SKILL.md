# Abstract and Title

## Purpose

Improve discoverability and comprehension without turning the abstract into marketing copy or overstating the findings.

## Input

Bring any material already available: final or near-final manuscript, verified headline results, target venue, word limit, keywords, and contribution statement. If an essential item is absent, state the gap and proceed with a clearly labelled provisional plan rather than repeatedly asking for information that is already present.

## Workflow

1. Extract the research question, method, data/sample, comparator, primary outcome, strongest result, and practical or theoretical implication.
2. Check that the result is final, correctly rounded, and consistent with the main tables; distinguish exploratory from confirmatory findings.
3. Draft the abstract in a venue-appropriate sequence: context/problem; gap/question; approach; key quantified result; interpretation and boundary.
4. Write title candidates that name the method/task/population or contribution without unsupported superlatives.
5. Remove generic opening claims and unexplained acronyms; make the abstract understandable without the full paper.
6. Verify every number, dataset name, baseline, confidence interval, and causal verb against the manuscript.
7. Count words using the venue’s rules and produce a compliant version plus a plain-language alternative if useful.

## Required output

Three title candidates with rationale; one venue-compliant abstract; word count; keywords; and a fact-check table mapping each factual statement to its manuscript source.

## Integrity and safety gates

- Do not invent a result to make the abstract more compelling.
- Do not omit a major adverse or null result if it changes the headline interpretation.
- Do not use “first”, “novel”, “state-of-the-art”, or “significant” without evidence and a defined comparison.
- Do not claim clinical, policy, or deployment impact from a purely offline benchmark.
