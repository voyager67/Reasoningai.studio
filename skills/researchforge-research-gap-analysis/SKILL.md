# Research Gap Analysis

## Purpose

Avoid novelty-by-assertion. A useful gap is both supported by the literature map and important enough to justify new work.

## Input

Bring any material already available: research question, search log, evidence matrix, candidate methods, data access, and compute/time limits. If an essential item is absent, state the gap and proceed with a clearly labelled provisional plan rather than repeatedly asking for information that is already present.

## Workflow

1. Define the literature boundary and date of coverage. State disciplines, venues, languages, and databases included.
2. Map what is known by task, population, method, dataset, metric, and context.
3. Classify candidate gaps: under-studied setting; weak measurement; missing comparator; conflicting evidence; untested mechanism; limited external validity; or poor reproducibility.
4. For each gap, cite multiple supporting records or explain why one foundational source is decisive. Distinguish observed absence from unsearched space.
5. Test whether the proposed gap is already addressed by adjacent terminology or a different field.
6. Score opportunities on significance, tractability, data/compute access, evaluation quality, and likely contribution; explain the scoring rather than hiding it in a total.
7. Convert the best opportunity into a question, hypothesis, method, and a disconfirming result.

## Required output

Gap map with evidence anchors, confidence, search boundary, existing partial solutions, opportunity ranking, feasibility notes, and one or more testable research questions.

## Integrity and safety gates

- Never claim “no one has studied this” without a broad, documented search.
- Do not call a new model application a methodological contribution unless the method itself changes.
- Separate novelty, usefulness, and feasibility.
- Explicitly include work that weakens the proposed novelty claim.
