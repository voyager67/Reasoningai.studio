# Research Question Framing

## Purpose

Create a question that is important, tractable, and capable of being answered with the available methods and resources. Do not confuse a topic, a product idea, and a research question.

## Input

Bring any material already available: topic or problem statement, target population or system, domain constraints, available data or compute, intended contribution, and any deadline. If an essential item is absent, state the gap and proceed with a clearly labelled provisional plan rather than repeatedly asking for information that is already present.

## Workflow

1. Restate the topic in one sentence and identify the decision or uncertainty the research should resolve.
2. Separate the phenomenon, population/system, context, intervention or method, comparator, and outcome. Use PICO/PECO for intervention questions, SPIDER for qualitative or mixed-methods questions, and a task–data–metric framing for ML.
3. Define key terms operationally. Replace vague outcomes such as “better reasoning” with observable measures and a measurement protocol.
4. Draft one primary question plus at most three subordinate questions. Specify the unit of analysis and the boundary conditions.
5. Apply a feasibility screen: data access, ethics, equipment/compute, baseline availability, time, skill, and evaluation cost.
6. List assumptions, plausible alternative explanations, and what evidence would make the question worth answering.
7. Write inclusion and exclusion boundaries before searching; mark anything still undecided as provisional.

## Required output

A one-page research brief with: problem; primary question; motivation; scope; definitions; target population/system; outcomes and metrics; comparator; assumptions; feasibility risks; ethics/data needs; and a short “what would change my mind?” section.

## Integrity and safety gates

- Do not label a question novel until a documented search supports that assessment.
- Do not promise causal conclusions from observational data.
- Do not expand the scope merely to make the project sound ambitious.
- Record unresolved assumptions instead of silently converting them into facts.
